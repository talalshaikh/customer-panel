# Reading the results

Read the **target** group first. The control segment pulls the overall numbers down on purpose.

## The four price answers (Van Westendorp)

Each person gave four prices for one unit, before seeing yours:
- too cheap (they'd doubt the quality);
- a bargain;
- getting expensive (they'd still consider it);
- too expensive (they'd never consider it).

From the share of people at each price, the tally reports:

- **Acceptable range:** from the point of marginal cheapness (PMC) to the point of marginal expensiveness (PME). Prices outside it lose people fast.
- **Optimal price point (OPP):** where as many people call it too cheap as too expensive.
- **Indifference price point (IPP):** where as many call it a bargain as call it getting expensive. This is roughly the "normal" price in people's heads.
- **Share who'd call each of your prices too expensive.** Usually the most useful single number, because it says how many people a price rules out.

A price above the acceptable range isn't automatically wrong. Packs and premium positioning can carry it. But it then has to be earned by the pitch, the offer and the comparison people make.

## Decisions

- **Buy** means anything but `none`. Trial-only buyers are mostly curiosity. Commitment (packs, courses, plans) is the real signal.
- **After the trial,** yes, maybe or no. Panels show far more "maybe" than real life converts, so treat maybe as no until proven otherwise.
- **The follow-up offer** shows what risk reducers and launch prices add on top of the list-price decision. If commitment only appears after the offer, the offer is doing the work, not the list price.

## What they compare it to

The tally lists each `compares_to` frame with its people, their median price answers, and how many bought. If buyers cluster in the premium frames, the job is positioning: move more people into that frame with words, pictures, venue and channel, rather than cutting the price.

A feature that pulls people into a cheap frame is a positioning risk, even if they enjoy it. One example is something that makes the product sound like an arcade game. Lead with it only in channels where that frame is fine.

## Objections

Count the tags, then read the `main_objection` text. The people's words are what you use in copy, FAQs and sales answers.

When a new tag appears right after a pitch change, it's a side effect of the change. For example, calling a product "training" can create "it's just getting good at a game". Answer it in the next pitch, or with something you build.

## What would make it worth more

- **Rank what to build.** Use the count and the median extra per unit.
- **Each person picked one addition,** so don't add the extras together.
- **The extra is relative to the person's own price answer from step 1,** not permission to raise your list price by that much. If people value a unit at 200 and you charge 270, an addition worth +50 closes most of that gap. It doesn't justify 320.

## Buying for others

- **Interest is not a purchase.** Look at budget per head against your price, the approval steps, and what they say they'd need: procurement, data terms, proof, reports, timing.
- **Weigh who can decide.** One "would buy" from a founder who signs alone is worth more than ten "tempted" from organisations with long procurement.
- **Look at the notes for what to offer instead.** People often say the offer they would buy, such as a different format, size or channel. Coaches or venues, for example, may want a referral fee.

## Noise

- With 25 people per segment, a change of 2–3 people between rounds is noise.
- For a combined target group of 75, changes under about 5 people are noise.
- Each round has new people, which adds more noise.
- Trust big moves (0 → 6 commitments) and patterns that repeat across rounds and segments.

## Report structure

1. **The answer** to the decision the user faces, in one or two sentences.
2. **A small table** of the key numbers against earlier rounds:
   - buyers;
   - commitment at list price;
   - commitment after the follow-up offer;
   - the target group's optimal price;
   - the share calling the main price too expensive.
3. **What changed and why**, with two or three short quotes.
4. **The `compares_to` table**, if it explains the price.
5. **The top objections** and the top additions.
6. **Buying for others:** what would sell, at what price, and through whom.
7. **Recommendation:** which prices and offers to keep, change or test.
8. **The caveat:** simulated and directional. Then the real-money test that would confirm it (deposits, pre-sales, a launch event) and the one number to watch in it.

Keep it in plain language. Explain Van Westendorp in a line the first time. Don't use the people's quotes outside internal discussion.
